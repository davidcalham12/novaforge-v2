# Chapter 1: The Sensor Reading

Maya Chen walked into the archive room with the printouts still warm from her machine, her hands steady despite the tremor in her chest. Three years of mapping errors. Three years of coordinates that didn't align. And now, on the ground beneath Broadway, her survey had found something the maps had forgotten.

"It's complete," she said, spreading the ground-penetrating radar analysis across the table. The image showed a platform, clearly formed, unnaturally preserved. Two hundred feet of structure with no matching entry in the City Transit Authority's database. "Full station configuration. Tracks. Loading gauge. Everything."

Sarah Okafor leaned forward, pushing her glasses up. The CTA archivist had become Maya's unlikely ally over coffee and shared frustration. "I'll need to verify, but..." Sarah's finger traced the readings. "This is architectural, not geological noise?"

"Not a chance." Maya pulled up the cross-sections. "I ran the survey four times. Different equipment. Same signal."

That was when Robert Hartley walked in—Sarah had asked him to come without explaining. He was seventy-three, officially retired for a decade, and the last engineer who'd worked through the system's full expansion. He saw the printouts and stopped.

"Where was this taken?" he asked quietly.

"Under Broadway. Between stations. In the gaps where nothing should exist."

Robert sat down slowly. "I need to tell you something. When I was digitizing the construction records in '79, there was a project. Project 117. It was in the permits—City Council approval, 1962. I remember noticing the file had gaps. Missing architectural drawings. A construction suspension order with no explanation for the gap."

"I found those gaps," Sarah said. "All of them. The 1962 to '63 section is systematically absent. Not misfiled. Removed. Deliberately, or in that digitization you mentioned."

Maya watched them understand it simultaneously. Three independent sources, none of them connected. Her sensors in the earth below Broadway. Sarah's audit of what was missing from the archive. Robert's memory of paperwork that shouldn't have been gapped. All three pointing to the same impossible fact: a station that had been approved and constructed, then erased from every record except the ground itself.

"The City won't acknowledge this," Sarah said, not as a question.

"They'll deny the radar," Robert added. "They'll say it's geological, equipment error, interference."

"Then we document it," Maya said. "Everything. Before anyone decides it doesn't exist."

The three of them looked at the printouts—the ghost made visible by waves passing through bedrock. Outside, the city's official maps remained unchanged, complete and certain. But beneath Broadway, something was waiting to be found again.
