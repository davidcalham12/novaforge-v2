# Chapter 2: The Regulatory Limbo

Robert met them at his apartment in Evanston, surrounded by filing boxes labeled in his careful script. He looked older than Sarah remembered—the weight of institutional memory settling across his shoulders like dust.

"The station was never *decommissioned*," he said, pushing a manila folder across his coffee table. "That's the whole problem."

Maya leaned forward. "Explain that."

"In 1963, someone issued a suspension order on Project 117. No paperwork saying *why*—that's redacted. But suspension isn't demolition. Suspension means you stop active operations and seal the asset pending review." He pulled out a photocopied regulation, dated 1962. "Once suspended, the asset has a legal status. It exists. CTA couldn't just erase it from records without filing a decommissioning order with the Public Utilities Commission, and that would require explaining what they were decommissioning."

Robert tapped the folder. "So instead, in 1979, someone did something worse. They erased it preemptively. Destroyed the original paperwork. Made suspension invisible." He met their eyes. "A station suspended but not decommissioned is a liability that never dies. It's still CTA property. Still technically functional in the eyes of law, even if nobody remembers it exists. A ghost asset in regulatory limbo."

Sarah had already started. She'd spent three days in the Records Management office at CTA headquarters, watching the receptionist's patience wear thin as she requested disposal logs from 1979. Everything was archived or destroyed—official channels yielded nothing but dates and file codes for materials that no longer existed.

"It's not carelessness," Sarah told the group. "Destruction at that scale requires authorization. Someone filed the destruction orders. There's a paper trail somewhere."

That Friday, Maya and Sarah descended beneath Broadway with a 1950s surveyor's map and a hunch. The tunnel entrance was there, exactly where the GPR had suggested—sealed behind concrete and painted over decades ago. Brick outlined in dust where the marking had been. Maya photographed everything, hands steady. Sarah found fresh scratches on the concrete. Recent. Last year, maybe.

Someone else had been here.

They didn't get inside, but construction photographs fell into place. Robert produced a 1962 payroll stub showing engineers assigned to "Project 117—Construction Phase." Sarah found a *Chicago Tribune* article from October 1962—"CTA Upgrades Underground Infrastructure"—that mentioned a classified modernization project, deep in the business section. The fragments assembled into corroboration.

By Sunday, the pressure began. Robert's supervisor called him, voice tight. "Stop asking about closed files." Sarah's director suggested she focus on current assignments. Both delivered the same underlying message: *Stop.*

Maya received an email from her firm's legal department. A routine request—copy of her GPR report. They wanted to review it before she used any data in external projects. Routine, except the timing. Except the names copied on the message.

She recognized one: An attorney listed in CTA's general counsel's office.

The question wasn't whether they were being watched. The question was: *Why did it matter enough to notice?*
