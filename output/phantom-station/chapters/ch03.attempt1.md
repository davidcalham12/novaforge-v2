# Chapter 3: The Convergence

Sarah stood in the basement archive with a stack of photocopy boxes, her hands trembling slightly. The misfiled section wasn't hidden—it was simply mislabeled. Three boxes marked "Systems & Maintenance—1974–1978" actually contained the 1962–1963 construction records, shoved in sideways as if someone had tried to obscure them with proximity to unrelated files.

The first document she pulled was the City Council approval memo for Project 117, dated March 1962. The second was the full construction permit set. The third was the blueprint—twelve pages of engineering drawings that matched Maya's GPR survey with impossible exactness. Platform dimensions, track bed specifications, tunnel cross-section. Everything. Her phone call to Maya that afternoon was nearly silent on Maya's end, just breathing.

"It was real," Sarah whispered. "All of it was real."

But the deeper story lived in the disposal manifests from 1979. Sarah had spent two days cross-referencing warehouse transfers with digitization schedules, and the pattern was unmistakable. A carton labeled "Project 117—Archived Construction (1962–1963)" had been logged as received during the Great Digitization, then immediately marked for disposal on a manifest dated November 15, 1979. The handwriting signature belonged to Richard Donnelly, the CTA supervisor who oversaw the migration. Donnelly retired three weeks later, with no forwarding address.

Someone had ordered the destruction. The signature made it deliberate.

What Sarah found in the permits solved the third mystery. There was no decommissioning order for Project 117. None. The suspension memo from August 1963 sat alone in the file—no closure paperwork, no resumption order, nothing. The station was suspended mid-construction and left in legal limbo, neither officially opened nor formally closed. That gap was the mechanism that allowed the concealment. No decommissioning paperwork meant no audit trail. The station didn't exist in any category that required oversight.

Robert, reading the documentation over her shoulder in the archive's back office, went very still. "Donnelly was junior then," he said quietly. "Not someone who would order something like this on his own. Someone told him to do it."

"Then that person," Sarah said carefully, "is still making sure it doesn't become a problem."

The three of them met with the CTA's Records Director the following Tuesday. Maya brought her GPR reports and the blueprint cross-reference. Robert brought his engineer's credentials and his forty-five years of institutional knowledge. Sarah brought the permits, the destruction manifest, and the regulatory audit.

The Records Director read the disposal order twice. "This shouldn't exist," she said finally.

"Neither should the station," Maya replied. "But it does."

By Friday, the CTA issued a press release acknowledging Project 117 as an official, documented subway station—suspended in 1963, left in regulatory limbo, and physically verified through archaeological survey. The documentation that should have proven its existence for sixty years was restored to the archive. Donnelly's name was not mentioned.

Maya's credibility stood. Sarah's integrity was vindicated. Robert's memory was confirmed.

And The Phantom Station became official record.
