# Story Bible: The Phantom Station

## Physical Setting

The story takes place in a contemporary North American city with a sprawling metropolitan subway system constructed over more than eighty years. Hundreds of stations remain active; dozens have been abandoned, closed, or repurposed. The city sits on bedrock suitable for tunneling, with extensive networks running beneath downtown, industrial zones, and residential corridors. Tunnels are accessible to authorized personnel through maintenance shafts, ventilation systems, and sealed station entrances. The physical infrastructure—tracks, platforms, tiled walls, signage—persists unchanged in abandoned stations; decay and water damage occur naturally over decades.

## The Cartography System

The official record is maintained by the City Transit Authority's engineering archives: architectural blueprints, construction permits, municipal approvals, and decommissioning records. A parallel system exists in academic libraries, historical societies, and independent cartography projects: these maintain maps built from public records, interviews with retired engineers, and field surveys. Digital mapping (GIS) systems now supplement both, but discrepancies between sources are attributed to lost documents, data entry errors, or imprecise historical records—not to undocumented infrastructure.

## Factions

**City Transit Authority (CTA):** Official stewards of the subway system. They maintain the canonical record, control access to tunnels, and decide which stations are "real" by documentation standards. Resistant to claims of missing stations; their authority depends on completeness of the archive.

**Independent Cartographers Guild (ICG):** Researchers, historians, and amateur documentarians who map the system through open records, field surveys, and oral history. They operate outside institutional authority, publishing findings in blogs, self-published books, and academic journals. Credibility depends on rigorous sourcing and consistency with official records where they exist.

## Technologies

**Ground-penetrating radar (GPR) and acoustic surveying:** Detects structural anomalies—walls, tracks, chambers—beneath the surface. Cannot distinguish between constructed and unfinished infrastructure without corroborating evidence.

**Digital cartography and GIS mapping:** Tools for integrating multiple data sources, cross-referencing coordinates, and visualizing discrepancies between maps. Precision is limited by the accuracy of underlying source documents.

**Physical archives:** Building permits, construction photos, worker rosters, payroll records, newspaper clippings. Incomplete; files are lost, water-damaged, or destroyed during relocations.

## Timeline Anchors

- **1920s–1980s:** Era of major subway expansion; most documentation survives but is scattered across multiple institutions.
- **1990s–2010s:** Digitization projects begin; some records are lost in format conversions or during building moves.
- **Present day:** Three generations of engineers have passed through the system; oral history is fragmenting.

## Rules

1. **A documented station must exist in at least two independent archival sources** (official blueprints OR construction permits, AND municipal records OR period newspapers) to be officially recognized as built. Undocumented stations cannot be claimed without evidence that documents were destroyed, not that construction never occurred.

2. **Ground-penetrating radar reveals only physical structures that exist, not plans, proposals, or intentions.** A chamber detected underground does not prove it was ever formally opened as a station or served passengers.

3. **Station decommissioning is irreversible in official city records.** Once a station is legally closed, it cannot be reopened as a "new discovery"; it can only be reclassified if original documentation is found proving it was never officially decommissioned.

4. **Names in the independent cartography record must match official city naming conventions to claim they refer to the same location.** A tunnel entrance called "Platform A" in field notes cannot be matched to "Station 7" in official records without documentary evidence.

5. **Institutional memory is fallible; documents are not.** An engineer's recollection that a station was never built cannot override contemporary written approval, even if that approval appears incomplete or contradictory.
